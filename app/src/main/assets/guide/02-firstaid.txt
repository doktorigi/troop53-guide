=== firstaid | First Aid | ⛑️ | #B5332A | Hurry cases, injuries, heat, cold, bites and more

# emergency-steps | Emergency Action Steps | Check, Call, Care
When someone is hurt, stay calm and follow these steps in order.
1. Check the scene. Is it safe for you? Watch for traffic, fire, falling rock, water, electricity, or angry animals. Do not become a second victim.
2. Check the person. Tap their shoulder and shout, "Are you okay?" Look for breathing and severe bleeding.
3. Call for help. Send a specific person: "You in the blue shirt, call 911 and come back." Get an adult leader.
4. Care for the person. Treat the hurry cases first, then other injuries.
5. Protect yourself. Wear gloves from the first aid kit when touching blood or body fluids, and wash your hands afterward.
! Do not move an injured person unless they are in danger where they are.
> Know where the first aid kit is on every outing, and know who has first aid or CPR training.

# hurry-cases | Hurry Cases | Life threats to treat first
Some injuries can kill within minutes. Treat these first, in this order of priority.
- Not breathing or no heartbeat: start CPR and get an AED.
- Severe bleeding: press hard on the wound.
- Choking: give abdominal thrusts.
- Heart attack or stroke: call 911 right away.
- Poisoning or serious allergic reaction: call 911; help with an epinephrine auto-injector if they have one.
- Shock: can come with any serious injury.
! Call 911 first for any hurry case, or send someone to call while you help.

# cpr | CPR and AED | When someone is not breathing
CPR keeps blood moving to the brain until help arrives. Take a CPR class; this is a reminder, not training.
1. Make sure the scene is safe. Tap and shout. If no response and they are not breathing or only gasping, call 911 and get an AED.
2. Place the person on their back on a firm surface.
3. Put the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, the other hand on top. Lock your elbows, shoulders over your hands.
4. Push hard and fast: about 2 inches deep, 100 to 120 times a minute. Let the chest come all the way back up.
5. If trained, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions: tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and breathe until the chest rises.
6. If not trained or not willing to give breaths, do hands-only CPR: keep pushing without stopping.
7. When the AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice prompts. Stand clear when it says to.
8. Keep going until the person starts breathing, an AED or trained help takes over, or you are too exhausted to continue.
> Push to the beat of "Stayin' Alive" to get the right speed.

# choking | Choking | Abdominal thrusts
A person who is choking cannot cough, talk, or breathe, and may grab their throat.
1. Ask, "Are you choking? Can I help?" If they can cough hard, encourage them to keep coughing.
2. If they cannot breathe or speak, have someone call 911.
3. Stand behind them. Make a fist with one hand and place it thumb side in, just above the belly button.
4. Grab your fist with your other hand and give quick, inward and upward thrusts.
5. Repeat until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive.
6. If they become unresponsive, lower them to the ground and start CPR. Look in the mouth for the object before giving breaths.
> Some courses teach 5 back blows between sets of abdominal thrusts. Follow your training.
! If you choke when alone, press your upper belly hard against the back of a chair or counter.

# bleeding | Severe Bleeding | Stop the bleed
Heavy bleeding can kill in minutes. Act fast.
1. Put on gloves if you can. Do not wait if you have none; use a barrier like a plastic bag or cloth.
2. Press directly on the wound with a clean cloth or gauze, hard, with both hands.
3. Keep pressing. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top. Do not remove the first layer.
4. When bleeding slows, wrap a bandage firmly over the dressing.
5. Call 911 for bleeding that will not stop.
6. For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that does not stop with pressure, a trained person may apply a tourniquet 2 to 3 inches above the wound. Note the time it was applied.
! Treat for shock. Keep the person lying down and warm.

# shock | Shock | When the body's circulation fails
Shock can follow any serious injury, burn, blood loss, or allergic reaction. It can kill even if the injury itself is not deadly.
## Signs
- Pale, cool, clammy skin
- Fast, weak pulse and fast breathing
- Restless, confused, weak, or dizzy
- Nausea, vomiting, thirst
## Treatment
1. Call 911.
2. Have the person lie down on their back.
3. If there is no injury to the head, neck, back, or legs, raise the feet about 12 inches.
4. Keep them warm with a blanket or jacket under and over them.
5. Do not give anything to eat or drink.
6. Stay with them, keep them calm, and keep checking their breathing.

# burns | Burns | Cool, cover, and get help
## First-degree (red skin, like a mild sunburn)
Cool with cool running water for 10 to 20 minutes, or until the pain eases.
## Second-degree (blisters)
1. Cool with cool water for at least 10 minutes.
2. Do not break blisters.
3. Cover loosely with a sterile, non-stick dressing.
4. Get medical help if the burn is large or on the face, hands, feet, or groin.
## Third-degree (charred, white, or leathery skin; may not hurt)
1. Call 911.
2. Do not remove clothing stuck to the burn.
3. Cover loosely with a clean, dry cloth.
4. Treat for shock.
! Never put butter, grease, ointments, or ice on a burn.
> Stop, drop, and roll if your clothing catches fire.

# cuts | Cuts and Scrapes | Small wounds
1. Wash your hands and put on gloves.
2. Press with a clean cloth until bleeding stops.
3. Rinse the wound with clean water. Gently remove dirt.
4. Apply antibiotic ointment if the person is not allergic.
5. Cover with an adhesive bandage or sterile dressing.
6. Change the bandage daily or whenever it gets wet or dirty.
! Get medical help if the cut is deep, gaping, from an animal or rusty object, or shows signs of infection: redness that spreads, warmth, swelling, pus, or fever.

# blisters | Blisters | Hot spots and blister care
A hot spot is a sore, red area that warns you a blister is coming. Stop and treat it right away.
## Hot spot
- Take off your boot and sock.
- Cover the area with moleskin, blister tape, or a gel pad.
- Fix the cause: wrinkled socks, wet feet, loose laces, or a small rock.
## Blister
- If it is small and not broken, protect it with a doughnut-shaped pad of moleskin around it.
- If it has broken, clean it, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover it.
> Prevent blisters with broken-in boots, wool or synthetic socks, and dry feet.

# sprains | Sprains and Strains | Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation
A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament at a joint, often an ankle or wrist. A strain is a pulled muscle.
Remember RICE:
- Rest: stop using the injured part.
- Ice: apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth for up to 20 minutes at a time.
- Compression: wrap with an elastic bandage. Not too tight; check that fingers or toes stay warm and pink.
- Elevation: raise the injury above the heart if you can.
! If the person cannot put weight on it, or it looks deformed, treat it like a fracture.

# fractures | Fractures and Splints | Broken bones
## Signs
- Pain, swelling, bruising
- Cannot move or put weight on it
- The part looks bent or deformed
- A snap was heard or bone shows through the skin (open fracture)
## Treatment
1. Do not try to straighten the bone. Keep the person still.
2. Call for help.
3. For an open fracture, cover the wound with a sterile dressing. Do not push the bone back in.
4. Splint it as you found it. The splint should reach past the joints above and below the break.
5. Pad the splint with cloth and tie it in place, not too tight. Check fingers or toes for warmth and color.
6. For a forearm or wrist, use a sling to support the arm.
7. Treat for shock.
> Splints can be made from sticks, rolled newspaper, trekking poles, or a sleeping pad.

# head-spine | Head, Neck and Back Injuries | Do not move them
Suspect a head, neck, or spine injury after a fall from height, a diving accident, a car crash, or a hard hit to the head.
1. Call 911.
2. Tell the person not to move.
3. Hold their head still in the position you found it, with your hands on both sides.
4. Keep them warm and watch their breathing.
5. Only move them if they are in danger where they are, and then move them as a unit with several helpers.
## Concussion signs
- Headache, confusion, dizziness, or memory loss
- Nausea or vomiting
- Blurry vision or sensitivity to light
- Unequal pupils or drowsiness
! Anyone with concussion signs must stop activity and be seen by a medical professional.

# hypothermia | Hypothermia | When the body gets too cold
Hypothermia happens when the body loses heat faster than it can make it. It can happen in summer too, especially when someone is wet, tired, and in wind.
## Signs
- Shivering, numbness, clumsiness
- Mumbling, confusion, bad decisions, the "umbles": stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles
- In severe cases, shivering stops and the person becomes very drowsy
## Treatment
1. Get the person out of wind and rain into a shelter or tent.
2. Remove wet clothes and replace them with dry clothing.
3. Put them in a sleeping bag with insulation underneath. Cover the head.
4. If they are alert and can swallow, give warm, sweet drinks and food.
5. Warm water bottles wrapped in cloth can go at the armpits, neck, and groin.
6. For severe hypothermia (no shivering, confused, unresponsive) call 911 and handle them gently.
> Prevent it: stay dry, dress in layers, eat and drink often, and watch your buddies.

# frostbite | Frostbite | Frozen skin
Frostbite most often affects fingers, toes, ears, nose, and cheeks.
## Signs
- Skin that is white, gray, or waxy and feels hard or numb
- Tingling or burning, then no feeling at all
## Treatment
1. Get to a warm place.
2. Warm the area with body heat, such as hands in armpits, or in warm (not hot) water.
3. Do not rub the area or use direct heat like a fire or heater.
4. Do not thaw it if there is a chance it will refreeze.
5. Get medical help.
! Treat for hypothermia first if the person is also very cold.

# heat | Heat Exhaustion and Heatstroke | Too hot is dangerous
## Heat exhaustion
Signs: heavy sweating, pale and clammy skin, headache, weakness, dizziness, nausea, muscle cramps.
1. Move to shade or a cool place.
2. Loosen clothing and have them lie down with feet raised.
3. Cool them with wet cloths and fanning.
4. Give sips of water or a sports drink if they are alert.
## Heatstroke: a hurry case
Signs: very hot skin that may be red and dry or still sweaty, confusion, strange behavior, fast pulse, collapse, seizure.
1. Call 911.
2. Cool the person immediately and fast. The best way is to immerse them in cold water up to the neck. Otherwise, soak them with water and fan hard, and put ice packs on the neck, armpits, and groin.
3. Do not give anything to drink if they are confused or unresponsive.
> Prevent heat illness: drink water often, rest in the shade, wear a hat, and hike early or late in the day.

# dehydration | Dehydration | Drink before you are thirsty
## Signs
- Dark yellow urine or not peeing much
- Thirst, dry mouth, headache
- Tired, dizzy, or cranky
## Treatment
- Rest in the shade and sip water or a sports drink.
- Small, frequent sips work better than chugging.
> Rule of thumb: if your pee is pale yellow, you are drinking enough.

# stings | Stings and Allergic Reactions | Bees, wasps, and anaphylaxis
## Bee and wasp stings
1. Scrape the stinger out with a fingernail or card edge. Do not squeeze it.
2. Wash the area with soap and water.
3. Apply a cold pack to reduce swelling.
## Anaphylaxis: a hurry case
A severe allergic reaction to stings, foods, or medicines. Signs include trouble breathing, swelling of the face, lips, or throat, hives all over, dizziness, or collapse.
1. Call 911.
2. Help the person use their epinephrine auto-injector (such as an EpiPen) in the outer thigh.
3. Keep them lying down unless they need to sit up to breathe.
4. A second dose may be needed if symptoms do not improve in 5 to 15 minutes and help has not arrived.
! Know which Scouts on your outing carry an epinephrine auto-injector and where it is kept.

# ticks | Tick Bites | Remove ticks safely
Ticks can carry Lyme disease and other illnesses. Check yourself, your buddy, and your gear every day.
1. Use fine-tipped tweezers to grab the tick as close to the skin as possible.
2. Pull straight out with steady pressure. Do not twist, squeeze, or burn it.
3. Wash the bite and your hands with soap and water.
4. Write down the date and where you were.
! See a doctor if you get a rash (especially a bull's-eye rash), fever, or body aches in the weeks after a bite.
> Prevent ticks: wear long pants tucked into socks, use repellent, and stay in the middle of trails.

# snakebite | Snakebite | Stay calm and get help
Most snakes are harmless, but treat every bite from an unknown snake as venomous.
1. Move away from the snake. Do not try to catch or kill it.
2. Call 911 or get the person to medical care as quickly as possible.
3. Keep the person calm and still. Movement spreads venom faster.
4. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing near the bite before swelling begins.
5. Keep the bitten part still and at about heart level.
6. Wash the bite gently and cover it with a clean dressing.
! Do not cut the bite, suck out venom, apply ice, or use a tourniquet.
> Prevent bites: watch where you put your hands and feet, use a flashlight at night, and never reach under rocks or logs.

# spiders | Spider and Scorpion Bites | Black widow, recluse and scorpions
1. Wash the area with soap and water.
2. Apply a cold pack.
3. Watch for signs of a serious reaction: muscle cramps, sweating, trouble breathing, or a wound that grows or darkens.
4. Get medical help for a black widow, brown recluse, or scorpion sting, or for any severe reaction.
> Shake out boots, clothes, and sleeping bags before using them.

# animal-bites | Animal Bites | Dogs, wildlife, and rabies
1. Get away from the animal.
2. Wash the wound well with soap and running water for several minutes.
3. Control bleeding and cover with a clean dressing.
4. Get medical help. Any bite from a wild animal or a bat could expose the person to rabies.
! Never touch or feed wild animals, even ones that seem friendly.

# poisoning | Poisoning | Swallowed, breathed, or touched
1. Get the person away from the poison if it is safe.
2. Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222, or 911 if they are unresponsive, having trouble breathing, or having a seizure.
3. Save the container or a sample of the plant or substance.
4. Do not make the person vomit unless a medical professional tells you to.
## Poison ivy, oak, and sumac
- Wash the skin with soap and cool water as soon as possible.
- Wash clothes and gear that touched the plant.
- Calamine lotion or hydrocortisone cream helps the itch. Do not scratch.

# nosebleed | Nosebleed | Lean forward, pinch
1. Have the person sit and lean forward, not back.
2. Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for 10 minutes without letting go.
3. A cold pack on the bridge of the nose can help.
! Get help if bleeding will not stop after 20 minutes or followed a hard blow to the head.

# eye | Object in the Eye | Dirt, sand, and chemicals
- Do not rub the eye.
- Flush with clean water, pouring from the inside corner toward the outside.
- For a chemical splash, flush for at least 15 minutes and call for help.
- If an object is stuck in the eye, do not remove it. Cover both eyes loosely and get medical help.

# sunburn | Sunburn | Prevention and care
- Prevent it: sunscreen SPF 30 or more, reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming, plus a hat and sunglasses.
- Treat it: cool the skin with cool water, apply aloe or moisturizer, and drink extra water.
- Severe sunburn with blisters, fever, or chills needs medical care.

# asthma-seizure | Asthma and Seizures | Two emergencies to know
## Asthma attack
1. Help the person sit up and stay calm.
2. Help them use their rescue inhaler.
3. Call 911 if breathing does not improve, lips turn blue, or they cannot speak.
## Seizure
1. Clear the area so they do not hit anything. Put something soft under the head.
2. Do not hold them down or put anything in their mouth.
3. Time the seizure. Call 911 if it lasts more than 5 minutes or is their first seizure.
4. When it ends, roll them onto their side and let them rest.

# first-aid-kit | Personal First Aid Kit | What to carry
Every Scout should carry a small personal kit.
- Adhesive bandages, various sizes
- Sterile gauze pads and adhesive tape
- Moleskin or blister pads
- Antibiotic ointment and soap or hand sanitizer
- Disposable gloves (nitrile)
- CPR breathing barrier
- Small scissors and tweezers
- Pain reliever approved by your parents
- Your own medications and a list of allergies
> Check your kit before every trip and replace what you have used.
